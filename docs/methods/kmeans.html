K-Means Clustering

Unsupervised · the workhorse for finding groups when you have no labels — partition the data into K clusters by minimizing within-cluster spread. Siblings: PCA (its natural partner — reduce dimensions/denoise then cluster, the exam pipeline), hierarchical clustering (groups without pre-setting K), and the supervised classifiers (LDA, logistic, trees) you should use instead the moment you actually have labels.

1. Essence

K-means partitions n observations into a pre-chosen number K of clusters so that points inside a cluster are as similar as possible and points in different clusters as dissimilar as possible (Lecture 6, slide 13/19). “Similar” means close in Euclidean distance, and the quantity it actually minimizes is the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS): the total squared distance from every point to the centroid of its assigned cluster. Each cluster is summarized by one point — its centroid \mu_i, the mean of the points assigned to it — and that centroid is precisely the location that minimizes squared distance to the cluster’s members (Lecture 6, slide 13/19). The output is a hard assignment: every point belongs to exactly one cluster, with no soft/probabilistic membership.

The algorithm alternates two cheap steps until the assignments stop changing: assign each point to its nearest centroid, then update each centroid to the mean of its newly assigned points (Lloyd’s algorithm). This always lowers WCSS and converges, but only to a local optimum — so the result depends on where the centroids started (Lecture 6, slide 15/19). Two standard guards address that fragility: k-means++ spreads the initial centroids out cleverly instead of picking them at random, and running the whole thing multiple times from different seeds (n_init) and keeping the lowest-WCSS solution. Both are on by default in scikit-learn.

The two design decisions that make or break a K-means analysis are choosing K and trusting the assumptions. K is not learned — you supply it — so the course teaches two tools to choose it: the Elbow method (plot WCSS against K and look for the kink where extra clusters stop paying off) and Silhouette analysis (a score in [-1,1] measuring how much better each point fits its own cluster than the next-nearest one). And K-means assumes spherical, roughly equal-sized clusters and is sensitive to outliers (a single far-flung point can drag a centroid toward it), because it is built on squared Euclidean distance (Lecture 6, slides 15/17). Compared with PCA — the other unsupervised method in this course — PCA re-describes the data along variance-maximizing axes (continuous, no groups), whereas K-means carves it into discrete groups; they combine beautifully, PCA first to denoise and shrink dimensions, K-means second to cluster (Lecture 6, slide 18/19).

2. When to use / when not to use

Reach for K-means when…

  • You have no labels and want to discover groups / segments — market or customer segmentation, document grouping, image-color quantization (Lecture 6, slide 16/19). The exam’s customer-segmentation task on WholesaleCustomers is exactly this (see §7).
  • You expect clusters that are roughly round and similar in size, and your features are continuous and on comparable scales (or you scale them first).
  • You want something fast and scalable — K-means is linear in n per iteration and handles large datasets where pairwise-distance methods choke.
  • You can pair it with PCA: reduce to a few components to denoise and visualize, then cluster — the course’s recommended combination (Lecture 6, slide 18/19).

Reach for something else when…

  • You actually have labels. Clustering ignores them; if the goal is to predict a known class, use a supervised model — LDA, logistic regression, decision trees. (The Iris example in §4 is a cautionary tale: the “best” K by silhouette is 2, not the 3 true species.)
  • Clusters are non-spherical, elongated, or very different in size / density → K-means’ equal-variance, round-blob assumption fails (Lecture 6, slide 17/19); reach for density- or shape-aware methods.
  • You don’t want to commit to K up front → hierarchical clustering builds a dendrogram you can cut at any level, no pre-set K required.
  • Outliers dominate → squared distance lets one stray point hijack a centroid (Lecture 6, slides 15/17); detect/trim outliers first, or use a robust alternative.
  • Features are wildly different in scale → the largest-unit feature dominates the Euclidean distance; standardize first (this is the most common K-means mistake, and the reason the exam pipeline scales before clustering — see §3).

3. Code idiom

The canonical course idiom is scikit-learn’s KMeans; the course does not build it from scratch — WCSS and the silhouette are introduced as formulas, and the clustering itself is always sklearn.cluster.KMeans (notebook 11_K_means.ipynb, cells 2, 8, 10).

Fit and read the labels (notebook 11_K_means.ipynb, cell 2):

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

# n_init = number of random restarts (best by WCSS is kept) — guards against bad init
kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=3, random_state=0, n_init=10).fit(X)
kmeans.labels_            # hard cluster assignment (0..K-1) for each point
kmeans.cluster_centers_   # the K centroids μ_i
kmeans.inertia_           # the final WCSS (sum of squared point-to-centroid distances)

Every hyperparameter that matters: n_clusters is K, the number of clusters (no default worth relying on — you must choose it, see below); init defaults to 'k-means++' (smart spread-out initialization — leave it); n_init is how many restarts to run (the notebook uses 10); max_iter caps Lloyd iterations (default 300); random_state fixes the seed for reproducibility. The one attribute you will reuse constantly is .inertia_ — that is the WCSS, and it is what the elbow method plots.

Choosing K — elbow (WCSS) + silhouette, in one loop (notebook 11_K_means.ipynb, cell 8, verbatim):

from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

wcss = []
silhouette_scores = []
for k in range(2, 10):
    kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=k, init='k-means++', max_iter=300, n_init=10, random_state=0)
    kmeans.fit(X)
    wcss.append(kmeans.inertia_)                              # elbow curve
    silhouette_scores.append(silhouette_score(X, kmeans.labels_))  # quality curve

WCSS (inertia_) always falls as K grows, so you look for the elbow — the kink where it stops falling fast. The silhouette, by contrast, has a genuine maximum: the K that scores highest is the silhouette’s pick. They do not always agree with each other, nor with any “true” number of groups — which is the whole lesson of §4.

Standardize before you cluster (unless features already share a scale). K-means uses raw Euclidean distance, so a feature measured in thousands (annual spend) will dominate one measured in single digits. The lecture stresses normalization as preprocessing (Lecture 6, slide 13/19 prose); the notebook skips it on Iris only because petal length and width are already both in centimetres on a similar range (notebook 11_K_means.ipynb, cell 1 advises normalization; cell 2 does not scale — petal features are pre-comparable). On any real multi-unit dataset (the exam’s WholesaleCustomers), fit a StandardScaler first — ideally inside the same scale → PCA → K-means pipeline (§4).

4. Worked examples

Example 1 — Iris elbow + silhouette, and why “best K” ≠ “3 species”

The course clusters Iris on just two features — petal length and petal width (notebook 11_K_means.ipynb, cell 2), deliberately ignoring the Species label (clustering is unsupervised). Running the elbow/silhouette loop from §3 over K = 2 \ldots 9 gives (computed via the course .venv, random_state=0, reproducing 11_K_means.ipynb cell 8 on the petal features):

K WCSS (inertia_) Silhouette
2 86.39 0.765
3 31.37 0.661
4 19.48 0.611
5 13.92 0.588
6 11.04 0.578
7 9.21 0.579
8 7.68 0.590
9 6.54 0.590

The silhouette is highest at K=2 (0.765), so the notebook’s next cell selects two clusters — and here is the catch worth quoting. The cell’s comment says one thing and the code does another:

# Apply k-means clustering with 3 clusters (one for each species of iris)
kmeans = KMeans(n_clusters=2, random_state=0, n_init=10).fit(X)   # ← actually K=2

The comment is stale (copied from the earlier cell-2 that used n_clusters=3); the actual call is n_clusters=2 (notebook 11_K_means.ipynb, cell 10; see [disagreements NB-4]). This mismatch is a gift of a teaching point, not a bug to paper over: the silhouette-optimal K=2 is not the 3 true Iris species. On petal features, setosa sits in a tight, well-separated blob while versicolor and virginica overlap heavily — so two clusters (setosa vs. the-rest) score as “cleaner” than three. To see how the two choices compare against the (known, but withheld) species labels, score each clustering with the Adjusted Rand Index (computed via .venv):

Clustering Silhouette ARI vs. 3 species
K=2 (silhouette’s pick) 0.765 0.558
K=3 (the true count) 0.661 0.886

K=3 recovers the species almost perfectly (ARI 0.886 — only 6 of 150 flowers land in the “wrong” cluster, all on the versicolor/virginica border), yet scores a lower silhouette than K=2 (computed). Lesson: the silhouette measures geometric separation, not correctness. With no labels you would have shipped K=2; the moment you have labels you would not be clustering at all. This is the single most important caveat about choosing K (see the big-picture callout in §6).

Example 2 — clustering with a ground truth: seeds

The local seeds.csv (140 grain samples, 7 geometric features, with a type variety label) lets us check clustering against a known answer — the “clustering with ground truth” case (course_materials/Data/seeds.csv; [DATA-2]). Crucially, this copy contains two wheat varieties (70 each), not the three of the full UCI set — so unlike Iris, the true count is 2. After StandardScaler (these features span very different units — area ~15, asymmetry ~2 — so scaling is mandatory), the elbow/silhouette loop gives (computed via .venv, random_state=0):

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(seeds.drop(columns=["type"]))   # 140 × 7, scaled
# same elbow/silhouette loop as §3 → silhouette peaks at K=2 (0.440)

The silhouette peaks at K=2 (0.440), falling to 0.299 at K=3 — and here the silhouette’s pick agrees with the ground truth. The K=2 clustering matches the two varieties well: ARI 0.758, with only 9 of 140 grains misassigned (computed). This is the happy mirror image of Iris: when the natural geometric grouping is the labelled grouping, silhouette and ground truth coincide. The contrast between the two examples — Iris (silhouette disagrees with labels) and seeds (silhouette agrees) — is exactly why you cannot treat the silhouette-optimal K as automatically “the right answer.”

Example 3 — the exam pipeline: scale → PCA → K-means on Wholesale customers

The Spring-2025 exam’s clustering task (Assignment 2) is customer segmentation on WholesaleCustomers.csv — 440 clients, 6 annual-spend categories (Fresh, Milk, Grocery, Frozen, Detergents_Paper, Delicassen) (course_materials/Lecture slides/Past exams/.../WholesaleCustomers.csv). The exam pipeline is standardize → PCA → K-means with elbow + silhouette to choose K — PCA both denoises and lets you visualize the clusters in 2-D:

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.decomposition import PCA
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(wholesale[spend_cols])   # 6 spend features, scaled
Xp = PCA(n_components=2, random_state=0).fit_transform(X)    # project to PC1, PC2
# elbow/silhouette loop on Xp → choose K

On the scaled data, PCA needs 4 components for ≥90 % of the variance, while the first two alone capture 72.5 % — enough for a 2-D segmentation view (computed; cf. the PCA page and Asgmt 2’s “90 % variance” sub-task). Clustering on the PC1–PC2 projection, the silhouette is highest at K=2 (0.636), with K=3 a close second (0.627) and a clear elbow in WCSS around K=3–4 (WCSS: 1227.9, 891.0, 612.8, 427.2, … for K=2 \ldots 5; computed via .venv). The honest read: K=2 separates the two dominant buyer types (the Channel/Region columns, withheld from clustering, broadly correspond to Horeca vs. Retail), while K=3 is defensible from the elbow — a textbook case where elbow and silhouette nudge you toward different K and you justify the choice rather than read it off mechanically (§7).

6. Diagnostics & interpretation

What to report and inspect to judge a K-means clustering:

  • The elbow curve (WCSS vs K) — inertia_ for each K; pick the kink where the marginal drop flattens. It is a guide, not a rule: the elbow is often ambiguous (Wholesale’s sits between 3 and 4).
  • The silhouette curve (and per-cluster silhouettes) — the K with the highest mean silhouette is the silhouette’s pick; values near 1 mean tight, well-separated clusters, near 0 mean overlap, negative mean misassigned points. Inspect the per-cluster silhouette widths, not just the mean — one good cluster can mask one bad one.
  • Centroids and cluster sizes — read cluster_centers_ back in the original feature units to name the segments (“high-grocery, low-fresh” buyers); check that sizes aren’t wildly imbalanced (a tiny cluster may just be outliers).
  • Stability across restarts and seeds — because K-means finds only a local optimum, refit with several random_state values; assignments that flip a lot signal an ill-posed K or non-spherical structure.
  • Against any held-out labels (if they exist) — score with the Adjusted Rand Index, as in Examples 1–2. But remember: if you have labels, clustering was the wrong tool.

Elbow vs. silhouette vs. ground truth — the three can all disagree, and that’s the point. The elbow asks “where do extra clusters stop reducing spread?” (a within-cluster compactness question); the silhouette asks “how cleanly separated are the clusters?” (a between-vs-within question); the ground truth, when it exists, asks “do the clusters match the real categories?” On Iris these split three ways — silhouette says 2, elbow leans 3, truth is 3 — whereas on seeds all three agree on 2. There is no K-selection criterion that is always right, because clustering is an unsupervised judgement about structure, not a prediction with an answer key. And all of this presumes K-means’ core assumption — roughly spherical, equal-size clusters: when clusters are elongated, nested, or differ in density, every K-selection rule misleads, and the fix is a different algorithm, not a different K (Lecture 6, slide 17/19). Pair with PCA to denoise first, but heed the lecture’s caution — don’t reduce dimensions too much (slide 18/19).

7. Past-exam lens

How K-means shows up — this is core. In the Spring-2025 take-home (Assignment 2), K-means is the clustering half of an unsupervised pipeline on WholesaleCustomers.csv: standardize the spend features → PCA (including the “how many components for 90 % of variance” sub-task — the answer is 4; computed) → K-means, choosing K with the elbow method and silhouette analysis (§4). Expect to (i) write the KMeans(...).fit and silhouette_score idiom from §3 — with a StandardScaler first; (ii) run the elbow/silhouette loop over a range of K and plot both; (iii) choose and justify K — the graded skill is the reasoning when elbow and silhouette disagree (Wholesale: silhouette 2, elbow ~3), not a mechanical readout; and (iv) interpret the segments by reading the centroids back in spend units. The classic trap: forgetting to scale, so Fresh/Grocery (thousands) swamp the distance and the clusters become meaningless. The Iris caveat from §4 is the conceptual point examiners love — optimal K need not equal the number of true classes.

8. Math

Math is off. Use the Math toggle in the top bar to show the course-level derivation (and “full” for the rigorous version).

Mode A — course level. (Notation: K = number of clusters, S_i = the i-th cluster (a set of points), \mu_i = its centroid; this K/S_i/μ_i convention follows Lecture 6 — see notation table.) The course poses K-means as a single optimization, the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS). Partition the data into clusters \{S_1,\dots,S_K\} to \min_{S_1,\dots,S_K}\;\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{x\in S_i}\lVert x-\mu_i\rVert^2, where \mu_i=\frac{1}{|S_i|}\sum_{x\in S_i}x is the centroid (mean) of cluster S_i — defined on the slide as “the point in feature space minimizing squared distance to all points in its cluster” (Lecture 6, slides 13–14/19). This objective is exactly scikit-learn’s inertia_ (§3). Reading it: \lVert x-\mu_i\rVert^2 is squared Euclidean distance, so WCSS is the total squared spread of points around their cluster’s center; minimizing it makes within-cluster points as similar, and between-cluster points as dissimilar, as possible.

The course does not solve this in closed form — there is none. Instead it runs Lloyd’s algorithm, which alternates two cheap steps until the assignments stop changing: (a) assign each point to its nearest centroid (Euclidean distance), then (b) update each centroid to the mean of its newly assigned points. Each pass lowers WCSS, so it converges — but only to a local optimum that depends on the initial centroids (Lecture 6, slide 15/19). The course’s two guards against bad initializations: k-means++ seeds the starting centroids spread out (each new seed drawn with probability proportional to its squared distance to the nearest existing centroid — init='k-means++', the sklearn default), and multiple restarts (n_init, the notebook uses 10) keep the lowest-WCSS run (Lecture 6, slide 15/19; notebook 11_K_means.ipynb, cell 8). The full local-vs-global / convergence argument is in Mode B. See the math appendix.

Mode B — full rigor. (Anchored to ISL §12.4.1, eqs 12.15–12.18, and ESL §14.3.6, eqs 14.31–14.34.)

The pairwise form and why it equals the centroid form. ISL frames K-means as minimizing the total within-cluster variation, \operatorname*{minimize}_{C_1,\dots,C_K}\;\Bigl\{\sum_{k=1}^{K} W(C_k)\Bigr\}\qquad\text{(ISL 12.15),} and takes the most common choice of W(C_k) — the mean pairwise squared Euclidean distance inside a cluster, W(C_k)=\frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i,i'\in C_k}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bigl(x_{ij}-x_{i'j}\bigr)^2\qquad\text{(ISL 12.16).} This pairwise bookkeeping never names a centroid, yet ISL’s key identity (12.18) shows it is the same objective as the slide’s centroid form: \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i,i'\in C_k}\sum_{j}\bigl(x_{ij}-x_{i'j}\bigr)^2 \;=\; 2\sum_{i\in C_k}\sum_{j}\bigl(x_{ij}-\bar x_{kj}\bigr)^2\qquad\text{(ISL 12.18),} where \bar x_{kj}=\tfrac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i\in C_k}x_{ij} is the cluster mean — i.e. the centroid \mu_k. So ISL’s pairwise W(C_k) equals twice the slide’s per-cluster centroid sum \sum_{i\in C_k}\lVert x_i-\mu_k\rVert^2: the two forms have the same minimizer and differ only by the constant factor 2 in this normalization ([disagreements R9-4]; the slide/centroid form is primary here because it is what inertia_ computes). ESL writes the same equivalence with the cluster size N_k=|C_k| made explicit (ESL 14.31): the within-point scatter W(C)=\tfrac12\sum_k\sum_{C(i)=k}\sum_{C(i')=k}\lVert x_i-x_{i'}\rVert^2=\sum_{k}N_k\sum_{C(i)=k}\lVert x_i-\bar x_k\rVert^2.

Why Lloyd’s algorithm decreases WCSS — and only to a local optimum. ESL’s identity (14.32) is the engine: for any set of points S, \;\bar x_S=\arg\min_m\sum_{i\in S}\lVert x_i-m\rVert^2 — the mean is the unique minimizer of summed squared distance. This lets the joint problem be written over both assignments C and centers \{m_k\}, \min_{C,\{m_k\}}\;\sum_{k=1}^{K}N_k\sum_{C(i)=k}\lVert x_i-m_k\rVert^2\qquad\text{(ESL 14.33),} and minimized by alternating (Algorithm 14.1): (a) hold C fixed, set each m_k=\bar x_k (optimal by 14.32); (b) hold \{m_k\} fixed, reassign C(i)=\arg\min_{1\le k\le K}\lVert x_i-m_k\rVert^2 (ESL 14.34). This is coordinate descent: each step minimizes (14.33) over one block with the other fixed, so “each of steps 1 and 2 reduces the value of the criterion (14.33), so that convergence is assured” (ESL §14.3.6). But it is descent on a non-convex surface — the objective never increases, yet “the result may represent a suboptimal local minimum” depending on the random start (ESL §14.3.6). ISL makes the combinatorial source of the difficulty concrete: there are “almost K^n ways to partition n observations into K clusters … a very difficult problem to solve precisely,” so Lloyd’s algorithm only “provides a local optimum — a pretty good solution” (ISL §12.4.1, around eq 12.17). This is exactly why both texts insist on multiple random restarts, keeping the run with the smallest objective (ESL §14.3.6; ISL §12.4.1, where six restarts find objectives ranging 235.8–320.9 and the best is chosen) — and why scikit-learn defaults to k-means++ plus n_init restarts (§3).

The implicit isotropic-Gaussian model. ESL §14.3.7 (“Gaussian Mixtures as Soft K-means”) shows K-means is the hard limit of fitting a mixture of Gaussians with scalar covariance \sigma^2\mathbf I: the EM “responsibilities” become 0/1 assignments as \sigma^2\to0, and the two methods coincide (ESL §14.3.7). That is the precise sense in which K-means assumes spherical, equal-variance clusters — the round-blob assumption flagged in §2 and §6 is not a heuristic but this isotropic-Gaussian model in disguise. It also explains the natural pairing with PCA: projecting onto variance-maximizing axes first denoises and reshapes the data toward the spherical geometry K-means expects (Lecture 6, slide 18/19), and ties choosing K to the bias–variance view — larger K always lowers WCSS (less bias) but eventually carves noise, which is why the elbow/silhouette judgement of §4 cannot be read off the objective alone.
