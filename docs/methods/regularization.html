Regularization — Ridge, Lasso, Elastic Net

Linear family · OLS with a penalty on coefficient size — the fix you reach for when predictors are many, correlated, or rival the sample size. Three siblings, one idea: Ridge (L2) shrinks, Lasso (L1) shrinks and selects, Elastic Net mixes the two. They sit between plain OLS (the \lambda=0 limit) and dimensionality reduction by PCA; their tuning is pure cross-validation and their pay-off is read through the bias–variance lens.

1. Essence

Regularized regression is ordinary least squares with one extra clause in the objective: alongside “make the residuals small,” it also says “keep the coefficients small.” You add a penalty term to the least-squares cost, scaled by a strength \lambda, that grows with the size of the coefficients — so the fit now balances fidelity to the data against coefficient magnitude (Lecture 3, slide 13/20). Turning \lambda up trades a little bias for a large drop in variance: the coefficients stop chasing the noise in the training sample, and the model generalizes better. At \lambda = 0 you are back to OLS; as \lambda \to \infty every coefficient is crushed toward zero (Lecture 3, slide 19/20).

The three family members differ only in how they measure coefficient size. Ridge penalizes the sum of squared coefficients (the L2 norm); it shrinks everything smoothly toward zero but essentially never to zero, and it is the natural cure for multicollinearity because it shares a coefficient out across correlated predictors instead of letting them blow up against each other (Lecture 3, slide 14/20). Lasso penalizes the sum of absolute coefficients (the L1 norm); its geometry has corners, so the optimum often lands exactly on an axis — driving some coefficients to exactly zero and thereby performing automatic feature selection (Lecture 3, slide 15/20). Elastic Net uses a weighted blend of both, governed by a mixing ratio \alpha, to get Lasso-style sparsity while keeping Ridge-style stability when predictors are correlated (Lecture 3, slide 17/20).

Like OLS, all three are parametric linear models — fast, interpretable, one weight per predictor — but where OLS has no hyperparameters worth tuning, regularization lives or dies by the choice of \lambda (and, for Elastic Net, \alpha). That choice is made by cross-validation, and getting it right is the whole skill (Lecture 3, slide 19/20).

2. When to use / when not to use

Reach for regularization when…

  • Predictors are many, correlated, or rival the sample size (p \gtrsim n, or strong multicollinearity). This is exactly where plain OLS overfits — the Hitters baseline in §4 shows the symptom (train R^2 well above test R^2).
  • You want to select features automatically → Lasso (or Elastic Net) zeros out the irrelevant ones, giving a sparse, more interpretable model. The synthetic example in §4 shows Lasso recovering true-zero coefficients that OLS leaves as small non-zero noise.
  • Many small, genuinely-present effects, none worth discarding → Ridge keeps them all but shrinks them together, the right move under multicollinearity (Lecture 3, slide 14/20).
  • You have correlated predictors and want sparsity → Elastic Net: Lasso alone tends to arbitrarily pick one of a correlated group and drop the rest; Elastic Net keeps the group together (notebook 05_Regularised_regressions (1).ipynb, cell 13).

Reach for something else when…

  • p \ll n, predictors uncorrelated, you want unbiased inference → plain OLS is the \lambda=0 limit and is BLUE under Gauss–Markov; a penalty would only add bias for no variance gain.
  • The relationship is clearly non-linear → a penalty cannot fix a wrong functional form; use Decision Trees / Random Forests or another flexible learner.
  • You want to compress correlated predictors into a few uncorrelated directions rather than zero some out → PCA is the dimensionality-reduction alternative (and can be combined with regression).
  • Choosing among Ridge / Lasso / Elastic Net: Lasso struggles with correlated predictors — it picks one and zeros its neighbors, so the choice is unstable; Ridge keeps all of them; Elastic Net balances. See the big-picture callout in §6.

The single most common regularization bug — alpha is not \alpha. In the course’s math (Lecture 3) and most textbooks, \lambda is the penalty strength and \alpha \in [0,1] is the Elastic-Net L1/L2 mixing ratio. In scikit-learn the argument named alpha is the penalty strength \lambda — not the mixing ratio — and the mixing ratio is the separate argument l1_ratio. The course notebook states this explicitly: “in scikit-learn the parameter alpha is the same as we here call \lambda. What we call \alpha is called l1_ratio in scikit-learn” (notebook 05_Regularised_regressions (1).ipynb, cell 13). Mixing these up silently changes which model you fit. See [disagreements R9-13] and the notation table item 3.

Standardize before you penalize. A penalty on coefficient size is meaningful only if the coefficients are on a comparable scale — otherwise a predictor measured in small units gets an artificially large coefficient and is penalized harder for no good reason. Always fit a StandardScaler (or center+scale) inside the cross-validation pipeline so the scaling is learned on training folds only. Lecture 3 stresses this twice (slides 19–20). The penalty does not apply to the intercept \beta_0.

3. Code idiom

The canonical course idiom is scikit-learn’s Ridge, Lasso, and ElasticNet, all from sklearn.linear_model. They share OLS’s .fit / .coef_ / .predict API; the one new thing is the penalty argument alpha (=\lambda).

The three estimators — (notebook 05_Regularised_regressions (1).ipynb, cells 10, 12, 14):

from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge, Lasso, ElasticNet

ridge = Ridge(alpha=0.8)                       # alpha = λ (penalty strength); L2
ridge.fit(X_train, y_train); ridge.coef_

lasso = Lasso(alpha=0.1)                        # alpha = λ; L1 → can zero coefficients
lasso.fit(X_train, y_train); lasso.coef_

en = ElasticNet(alpha=0.1, l1_ratio=0.5)       # alpha = λ; l1_ratio = α (mix). 0.5 = even L1/L2 split
en.fit(X_train, y_train); en.coef_

Every hyperparameter, annotated: alpha is the penalty strength \lambda (default 1.0; larger ⇒ more shrinkage); l1_ratio is the Elastic-Net mixing ratio (the math’s \alpha; 0.0 ⇒ pure Ridge, 1.0 ⇒ pure Lasso, 0.5 ⇒ even split). The 0.8 / 0.1 / 0.1 values are the course’s teaching settings on the synthetic data — in practice you tune alpha by cross-validation (see below).

The Lasso path with LARS (notebook 05_Regularised_regressions (1).ipynb, cell 19) — Least Angle Regression computes the entire sequence of Lasso solutions as \lambda varies, in one pass, so you can see the order in which features enter:

from sklearn.linear_model import lars_path

_, _, coefs = lars_path(X_train, y_train, method="lasso", verbose=True)
xx = np.sum(np.abs(coefs.T), axis=1); xx /= xx[-1]   # normalized L1 norm on the x-axis
plt.plot(xx, coefs.T, lw=2)                           # each line = one coefficient's trajectory

Tuning alpha by cross-validation, with scaling baked in (notebook 08_Information_criteria_and_cross_validation (1).ipynb, cells 6, 11, 12). The *CV estimators search a grid of alpha internally; wrapping them in a make_pipeline with StandardScaler guarantees the standardize-before-penalty rule and leaks no test information:

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.linear_model import LassoCV, LassoLarsCV, LassoLarsIC

# Cross-validated alpha (coordinate descent), 10 folds:
model = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LassoCV(cv=10)).fit(X, y)
model[-1].alpha_          # the selected λ;  .mse_path_, .alphas_ for the CV curve

# Cross-validated alpha (LARS solver), 20 folds — note .cv_alphas_ (not .alphas_):
make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LassoLarsCV(cv=20)).fit(X, y)

# Pick alpha by information criterion instead of CV (no hold-out needed):
ic = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LassoLarsIC(criterion="aic")).fit(X, y)
ic.set_params(lassolarsic__criterion="bic").fit(X, y)   # switch a pipeline step's param

Two small API traps from notebook 08. (i) LassoCV exposes its grid as .alphas_, but LassoLarsCV exposes it as .cv_alphas_ — a genuine attribute-name difference, not a typo. (ii) The notebook itself uses cv=10 for LassoCV but cv=20 for LassoLarsCV (cells 11–12), a small code/prose inconsistency — pick one fold count for a fair comparison. (notebook 08_..., [VERIFY] cv=20 intended for LARS — see notebook 08 extract.)

4. Worked examples

Example 1 — Lasso recovers the zeros that OLS only shrinks (synthetic)

The course builds a sparse data-generating process where the truth is known, so you can check which method recovers it (notebook 05_Regularised_regressions (1).ipynb, cell 3). The true coefficient vector is [5.5, 1.75, 1, 0, -2, 0, 0.6, 0, -9.6, 0] — features 3, 5, 7, 9 (0-indexed) have exactly zero true effect:

np.random.seed(15)
X = np.random.rand(100, 10)                     # 100×10, Uniform(0,1)
y = (5.5*X[:,0] + 1.75*X[:,1] + 1*X[:,2] - 2*X[:,4]
     + 0.6*X[:,6] - 9.6*X[:,8] + 5.0*np.random.randn(100))   # noise std 5
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, random_state=15)

Fitting all four models on this fixed draw, the coefficients tell the story (notebook cells 6, 10, 12, 14). OLS leaves the four true-zero features as small but non-zero noise (e.g. -2.247, -4.097, 0.898, -0.195 at indices 3, 5, 7, 9); Ridge shrinks every coefficient a little but still leaves all ten non-zero. Lasso (alpha=0.1) drives three coefficients to exactly 0. (indices 6, 7, 9), and Elastic Net zeros indices 6 and 9 — both performing the feature selection OLS and Ridge cannot. (Note: neither perfectly recovers the true zero-set {3,5,7,9} — features here are unscaled and the noise is large; this is itself the lesson that selection is imperfect under noise, and the cue to standardize.)

On the held-out test set the regularized models all beat OLS (notebook cell 16, stored outputs):

Model Test MSE (stored, nb05 cell 16)
Linear (OLS) 20.7316
Ridge (alpha=0.8) 19.9094
Lasso (alpha=0.1) 19.7805
Elastic Net (alpha=0.1, l1_ratio=0.5) 19.7715

OLS 20.73 > Ridge 19.91 > Lasso 19.78 > Elastic Net 19.77: a small but consistent improvement, with Elastic Net lowest here. The LARS path (cell 20, stored) confirms the ordering: feature 8 (true coefficient -9.6, the strongest effect) enters first, feature 0 (true 5.5) second — strongest effects enter earliest, as the §5 visualization shows.

Example 2 — Ridge and Lasso close the Hitters train–test gap

This continues the OLS page’s Hitters baseline — the Spring-2025 take-home (Assignment 1) regression task on Hitters.csv (263 players after dropping missing salaries, 16 numeric predictors, 75/25 split, random_state=1). OLS there fit train R^2 = 0.55 but test R^2 = 0.36 — a wide gap signalling variance from partly-redundant career-total predictors. We now add Ridge and Lasso with cross-validated alpha inside a scaling pipeline:

from sklearn.linear_model import RidgeCV, LassoCV
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

ridge = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), RidgeCV(alphas=np.logspace(-3, 3, 100))).fit(Xtr, ytr)
lasso = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LassoCV(cv=10, max_iter=100000, random_state=1)).fit(Xtr, ytr)

Results (computed via the course .venv; OLS row reproduces the OLS page):

Model Selected alpha (=λ) Train R^2 Test R^2 Test RMSE
OLS — 0.55 0.36 $377k
Ridge (CV) 2.48 0.54 0.39 $370k
Lasso (CV) 2.15 0.54 0.38 $373k

Both shrink the train R^2 slightly (0.55 → 0.54) while raising test R^2 (0.36 → 0.38–0.39) and lowering test RMSE — the train–test gap narrows from 0.19 to about 0.15. That is the bias–variance trade-off made concrete: a touch more bias, a useful cut in variance, better generalization (computed). Lasso additionally zeros 2 of the 16 coefficients (CAtBat, CRBI) — two correlated career totals it judges redundant given the others, exactly the feature-selection behaviour OLS lacks (computed). See the regression showdown for OLS vs Ridge vs Lasso vs Tree vs RF on this same dataset.

This is why the exam pairs OLS with regularization: the gain is modest in R^2 but the reasoning — diagnose variance, shrink, re-check the gap, interpret the surviving coefficients — is the graded skill.

Example 3 — choosing alpha by AIC/BIC and by cross-validation (diabetes)

To show how \lambda is selected (not guessed), notebook 08 takes the sklearn diabetes dataset (442 samples, 10 real features) and deliberately appends 8 random noise features — a clean test of whether selection drops the junk (notebook 08_..., cells 2–3):

X, y = load_diabetes(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True); X = X/X.std()
rng = np.random.RandomState(10)
X_random = pd.DataFrame(rng.randn(X.shape[0], 8), columns=[f"random_{i:02d}" for i in range(8)])
X = pd.concat([X, X_random], axis=1)            # 10 real + 8 noise = 18 features

Two selection routes, both inside make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), ...):

  • Information criteria (cell 6): LassoLarsIC(criterion="aic"), then set_params(lassolarsic__criterion="bic"). AIC (=2k - 2\ln L) and BIC (=k\ln n - 2\ln L) each pick the alpha minimizing the criterion; BIC’s heavier penalty (\ln n per parameter) prefers a sparser model. Read off alpha_aic = ...[-1].alpha_ and the features sent to zero via np.where(coefs == 0)[0]. Reproducing the notebook (computed via the course .venv, RandomState(10)): AIC selects alpha ≈ 1.52 and keeps 8 of the 18 features — 7 of the 10 real features plus 1 noise feature (random_07) that survives; BIC selects the larger alpha ≈ 4.35 and keeps just 5 features — all 5 real, with none of the 8 noise features surviving. This is the textbook contrast made concrete: BIC’s stiffer \ln n penalty drives the sparser, cleaner selection (zero junk), while the lighter AIC lets one noise feature slip through.
  • Cross-validation (cells 11–12): LassoCV(cv=10) (coordinate descent) and LassoLarsCV(cv=20) (LARS). Each averages the MSE over folds for every alpha and picks the minimizing lasso.alpha_; the CV curve (mse_path_ per fold, mean in black, vertical line at the chosen alpha) is the canonical diagnostic. The information-criterion route is cheaper (no hold-out) but leans on the asymptotic AIC/BIC approximations; CV is more robust but costs V refits. See CV & Information Criteria for the full treatment.

6. Diagnostics & interpretation

What to report and inspect to judge a regularized model:

  • Train vs test R^2/MSE — the headline. Regularization should narrow the train–test gap relative to OLS even if train error rises slightly (Hitters: gap 0.19 → ~0.15). A gap that stays wide means \lambda is too small; train error that collapses toward test with both near-useless means \lambda is too large (underfitting).
  • The CV curve of error vs alpha — plot mse_path_ per fold with the mean and a line at the selected alpha (notebook 08, cells 11–12). The minimum is your \lambda; consider the 1-SE rule (the largest \lambda within one standard error of the minimum) for a simpler model (Lecture 3, slide 19/20).
  • The coefficient path / which features survive — for Lasso/Elastic Net, list the zeroed coefficients (np.where(coef_ == 0)[0], notebook 08 cell 6). The surviving set is the selected model; interpret it, but remember selection is unstable under correlation.
  • Coefficient magnitudes only after scaling — penalized coefficients are comparable across predictors only because you standardized; never read raw-scale penalized coefficients as effect sizes without that caveat.

Ridge vs Lasso vs Elastic Net — the choice in one breath. Ridge shrinks all coefficients, keeps every predictor, and is the multicollinearity specialist — but it never simplifies the model. Lasso zeros coefficients (built-in feature selection) but, faced with a group of correlated predictors, arbitrarily keeps one and drops the rest — unstable selection. Elastic Net blends them: enough L1 to get sparsity, enough L2 to keep correlated groups together. Decision rule from Lecture 3: many small effects / collinearity ⇒ Ridge; need a sparse interpretable model ⇒ Lasso; correlated predictors and want sparsity ⇒ Elastic Net. Versus the PCA alternative, regularization keeps the original (interpretable) feature axes; PCA replaces them with uncorrelated components.

7. Past-exam lens

How regularization shows up — this is core. In the Spring-2025 take-home (Assignment 1, on Hitters.csv), regularization is the heart of the regression task: fit OLS as the baseline, then Ridge and Lasso with cross-validated alpha, and interpret the change in coefficients and the bias–variance trade-off (phase0/exam_spring2025_notebook.md). Expect to (i) write the Ridge/Lasso idiom from §3 — with StandardScaler in a pipeline; (ii) tune alpha by cross-validation (LassoCV/RidgeCV or GridSearchCV) and justify the value, possibly with the 1-SE rule; (iii) report and interpret in/out-of-sample R^2/MSE, connecting the narrowed train–test gap to reduced variance; and (iv) read off which coefficients Lasso zeros and explain the feature selection. The grade rests on the justification — why shrinkage helps here — at least as much as the code. The classic slip to avoid: confusing sklearn’s alpha (=\lambda) with the Elastic-Net mixing \alpha (l1_ratio) — see the pitfall in §2.

8. Math

Math is off. Use the Math toggle in the top bar to show the course-level derivation (and “full” for the rigorous version).

Mode A — course level. Each family member is plain least squares plus a penalty on coefficient size; they differ only in how size is measured. The course writes all three with the same \tfrac{1}{2n} prefactor on the squared-error term and an un-penalized intercept \beta_0 (Lecture 3, slides 14/15/17; notebook 05_Regularised_regressions (1).ipynb markdown cells 9/11/13). Ridge penalizes the squared (L2) coefficients, J^{\text{ridge}}(\beta)=\tfrac{1}{2n}\sum_i\Big(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_j\beta_j x_{ij}\Big)^2+\lambda_r\sum_j\beta_j^2, Lasso penalizes the absolute (L1) coefficients, J^{\text{lasso}}(\beta)=\tfrac{1}{2n}\sum_i\Big(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_j\beta_j x_{ij}\Big)^2+\lambda_\ell\sum_j|\beta_j|, and Elastic Net takes a convex blend of the two, governed by the mixing ratio \alpha\in[0,1], J^{\text{EN}}(\beta)=\tfrac{1}{2n}\sum_i\Big(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_j\beta_j x_{ij}\Big)^2+\lambda_e\Big[(1-\alpha)\sum_j\beta_j^2+\alpha\sum_j|\beta_j|\Big], with \alpha=0\Rightarrow Ridge and \alpha=1\Rightarrow Lasso (Lecture 3, slide 17/20). Reading it: the penalty pulls every \beta_j toward zero; the squared L2 term shrinks smoothly and never quite reaches zero, while the kinked L1 term can set coefficients exactly to zero — the source of Lasso’s feature selection (the geometry behind this is in Mode B).

Choosing \lambda. The strength \lambda is not guessed but selected on held-out data: fit over a grid of candidate \lambda and pick the value minimizing the cross-validated error, or — for a simpler model — the largest \lambda whose CV error is within one standard error of the minimum (the 1-SE rule) (Lecture 3, slide 19/20; the LARS path of §5 and the IC route of Example 3 are the companion tools). The two endpoints anchor the range: at \lambda=0 the penalty vanishes and the objective is exactly the OLS sum of squares; as \lambda\to\infty the penalty dominates and every \beta_j\to0, so the fit collapses to the intercept-only model (Lecture 3, slide 19/20).

Convention notes. The \tfrac{1}{2n} prefactor and explicit un-penalized \beta_0 are the slide/sklearn convention; ESL §3.4 drops the \tfrac{1}{2n} and centers the data so \beta_0 vanishes — the two are equivalent up to a rescaling of \lambda ([disagreements R9-12]). And recall the notation trap: in this math \lambda is the penalty strength and \alpha is the EN mix, but scikit-learn’s alpha argument is \lambda (the mix is l1_ratio). Scikit-learn’s exact ElasticNet objective, \tfrac{1}{2n}\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2 + \lambda\cdot\texttt{l1\_ratio}\cdot\lVert\beta\rVert_1 + \tfrac12\lambda(1-\texttt{l1\_ratio})\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2, matches the slide form once you identify \texttt{l1\_ratio}=\alpha but writes the L2 term with a \tfrac12 factor ([disagreements NB-8]). See the MAP/Bayes and KKT treatment in the math appendix.

Mode B — full rigor. (Anchored to ESL §3.4 “Shrinkage Methods”: Ridge §3.4.1, Lasso §3.4.2, eqs 3.41–3.52; data assumed centered so the intercept is handled separately and \mathbf X has p columns.)

Ridge: penalized ⇔ constrained, and the closed form. ESL defines ridge by the penalized criterion \hat\beta^{\text{ridge}}=\arg\min_\beta\Big\{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^N\big(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_j x_{ij}\beta_j\big)^2+\lambda\sum_j\beta_j^2\Big\}\quad(\text{ESL 3.41}), which is equivalent to the size-constrained problem \min\sum_i(\cdot)^2 subject to \sum_j\beta_j^2\le t — there is a one-to-one correspondence between \lambda and t (ESL 3.42). The penalized form is the Lagrangian of the constrained form. In matrix notation \mathrm{RSS}(\lambda)=(y-X\beta)^\top(y-X\beta)+\lambda\beta^\top\beta (ESL 3.43); differentiating and setting to zero gives the closed form \hat\beta^{\text{ridge}}=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y\qquad(\text{ESL 3.44}). Why this is the multicollinearity cure: adding \lambda I (a positive constant on the diagonal) makes X^\top X+\lambda I nonsingular even when X^\top X is singular or near-singular — i.e. when columns are perfectly or nearly collinear and the OLS inverse (X^\top X)^{-1} blows up or fails to exist (ESL p64; Hoerl & Kennard, 1970). Geometrically (ESL 3.47, via the SVD X=UDV^\top) ridge shrinks the coordinate along the j-th principal direction by d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda), so the low-variance directions (small singular values d_j) — exactly the ill-determined ones — are shrunk most. The intercept \beta_0 is deliberately left out of the penalty: penalizing it would make the solution depend on the origin chosen for y, so one centers the inputs, estimates \beta_0=\bar y, and fits the rest without an intercept (ESL p64, Exercise 3.5).

Lasso: same constraint geometry, but L1 — and the exact zeros. Replacing the L2 ball by the L1 ball gives the lasso, \hat\beta^{\text{lasso}}=\arg\min_\beta\textstyle\sum_i\big(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_j x_{ij}\beta_j\big)^2\ \text{ s.t. }\ \sum_j|\beta_j|\le t\quad(\text{ESL 3.51}), equivalently the Lagrangian form \arg\min_\beta\{\tfrac12\sum_i(\cdot)^2+\lambda\sum_j|\beta_j|\} (ESL 3.52). Because |\beta_j| is non-differentiable at \beta_j=0, there is no closed form; the optimum is characterized by the KKT / sub-gradient conditions. For the orthonormal-design case ESL solves them exactly: ridge does proportional shrinkage \hat\beta_j/(1+\lambda) while lasso does soft-thresholding \operatorname{sign}(\hat\beta_j)\,(|\hat\beta_j|-\lambda)_+ (ESL Table 3.4) — the (\cdot)_+ truncates to exactly zero any coefficient whose unpenalized magnitude is below \lambda. That truncation is the algebraic statement of the geometry in ESL Figure 3.11: the elliptical RSS contours first touch the L1 diamond at a corner (where some \beta_j=0), whereas they touch the smooth L2 circle generically off-axis (no exact zeros) — Lasso’s corners are why it selects, Ridge’s roundness is why it merely shrinks (Lecture 3, slide 16/20, which credits this figure to ESL).

The Bayesian/MAP reading. ESL notes (p64) that ridge is the posterior mode (and mean) under a Gaussian model: with y_i\sim N(\beta_0+x_i^\top\beta,\sigma^2) and an independent Gaussian prior \beta_j\sim N(0,\tau^2), the negative log-posterior equals the ridge criterion (3.41) with \lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2 (ESL p64, Exercise 3.6). By the same logic, swapping in a Laplace (double-exponential) prior \propto e^{-|\beta_j|/b} yields the L1 penalty, so Lasso is the MAP estimate under a Laplace prior — the heavier-tailed, sharply-peaked-at-zero prior is what produces sparse modes. See the MAP-as-penalized-MLE and KKT entries in the math appendix and the OLS Mode B for the \lambda=0 baseline this generalizes.
