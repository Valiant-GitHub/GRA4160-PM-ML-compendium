k-Nearest Neighbors (kNN)

Linear family · the simplest possible supervised baseline — it stores the training set and classifies a new point by looking at who its neighbors are. Grouped here with the baseline supervised methods, but unlike the rest of the family it is non-parametric: it assumes no functional form at all. Siblings: Logistic Regression and LDA (parametric linear boundaries), Decision Trees (non-parametric, axis-aligned), OLS (the parametric regressor it is most often contrasted with).

1. Essence

k-Nearest Neighbors classifies a new point by a vote of its closest training examples: measure the distance from the query to every training point, keep the K nearest, and assign the label that the majority of those K hold (Lecture 2, slide 8/10). There is no “model” in the usual sense — nothing is fitted, no coefficients are estimated. The training set is the model; “fitting” just stores it. All the work happens at prediction time, when the distances are computed.

It is the textbook non-parametric method: it commits to no functional form and has no fixed number of parameters — the effective complexity grows with the data (Lecture 2, slide 9/10). That makes it flexible (it can carve out arbitrarily wiggly decision boundaries) and almost assumption-free, but also hungry for data, expensive to query, and exquisitely sensitive to two choices: how many neighbors to consult (K) and how distance is measured (the metric, and therefore the scale of the features).

The single knob, K, is the bias–variance dial made tangible. K too small → the boundary chases individual points → overfit, high variance. K too large → the vote washes out local structure toward the global majority → underfit, high bias (Lecture 2, slide 8/10). The course’s prescription is to choose K by cross-validation (Lecture 2, slide 8/10).

Compared with its nearest neighbors: OLS and logistic regression fix a form (a line/plane) and spend training time estimating a handful of coefficients, then predict instantly; kNN inverts that — instant “training,” expensive prediction, and a boundary that is local rather than global. Decision trees are the other non-parametric baseline, but they partition the space with axis-aligned cuts and ignore feature scale, whereas kNN measures raw distance and so demands that features be scaled.

2. When to use / when not to use

Reach for kNN when…

  • You want a fast-to-stand-up, assumption-free baseline for classification (it also does regression by averaging neighbors, Lecture 2 slide 8/10, but the course uses it as a classifier). It is the “first model” you try alongside Naive Bayes (Lecture 2, slide 8/10).
  • The decision boundary is irregular but locally smooth — kNN follows curvature that OLS/logistic cannot, without you specifying any form.
  • You have enough data relative to the number of features, and you can scale the features so distance is meaningful.
  • You want intuition you can point at: a prediction is literally “these K labeled examples nearby.”

Reach for something else when…

  • The feature space is high-dimensional → the curse of dimensionality makes “nearest” almost meaningless (all points become roughly equidistant). Use a parametric model (logistic/LDA), reduce dimensions first with PCA, or use a tree/random forest that selects features.
  • You need an interpretable global rule (“+1 unit of X ⇒ +β”) → kNN gives no coefficients; reach for OLS/logistic, or a decision tree for if-then rules.
  • Prediction must be cheap or the dataset is large → every query scans (a structure over) the whole training set; a fitted logistic or tree predicts in constant time (Lecture 2, slide 8/10: “computationally expensive for large data”).
  • Features are on wildly different scales and you can’t scale them → the largest-range feature silently dominates the distance (see §3 and the wine example in §4).
  • The boundary really is linear → a parametric linear model will match kNN with far less data and a readable boundary; this is the parametric-vs-non-parametric trade-off (Lecture 2, slide 9/10).

3. Code idiom

The course teaches kNN both from scratch (so the distance-and-vote mechanics are not a black box) and via scikit-learn’s KNeighborsClassifier. Both run on the same iris split with K = 3 and agree (§4).

Canonical (sklearn) — (notebook 03_Supervised_learning_with_kNN (1).ipynb, cell 12)

from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier

# Create a k-NN classifier with 3 neighbors
knn = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=3)   # n_neighbors = K, the only knob you must set
knn.fit(X_train, y_train)                     # "fit" just stores the training data
y_pred = knn.predict(X_test)                  # the work happens here, at predict time
knn.score(X_test, y_test)                     # mean accuracy on the test set

n_neighbors (the K of the algorithm) is the hyperparameter you tune — by cross-validation, per Lecture 2 slide 8/10. Everything else in the notebook is left at the sklearn default: weights='uniform' (every neighbor votes equally — 'distance' would weight closer points more), and metric='minkowski' with p=2, which is Euclidean distance — exactly the metric the course defines (notebook cell 3).

From scratch — distance, sort, vote (notebook 03_Supervised_learning_with_kNN (1).ipynb, cell 7). This is the whole algorithm in three small functions, and shows literally what predict does under the hood — compute every distance, take the K smallest, majority-vote:

import numpy as np

def euclidean_distance(x, y):
    """Calculate the euclidean distance between two points"""
    return np.sqrt(np.sum((x - y)**2))

def predict(X_train, y_train, X_test, k):
    """Find the k nearest neighbors of x_test in X_train"""
    distances = [euclidean_distance(X_test, x) for x in X_train]  # distance to every train point
    k_nearest = np.argsort(distances)[:k]                          # indices of the K smallest
    return y_train[k_nearest]                                      # their labels

def predict_label(X_train, y_train, X_test, k):
    """Find the most common label among the k nearest neighbors of X_test in X_train"""
    return np.bincount(predict(X_train, y_train, X_test, k)).argmax()  # majority vote

Used across the whole test set with k = 3 (cell 8):

y_pred = [predict_label(X_train, y_train, x, 3) for x in X_test]
accuracy = np.mean(y_pred == y_test)

Scaling is not optional for kNN. Distance treats every feature in its raw units, so a feature with a large numeric range dominates the vote regardless of how informative it is. The course notebook feeds unscaled iris features straight in (cell 4 — no StandardScaler), which is fine only because iris’s four features share a comparable scale (all cm). On a dataset with mismatched ranges it silently breaks: on wine, proline (std ≈ 314) swamps features with std ≈ 0.12, and raw-feature kNN scores 68.89% versus 97.78% once standardized (§4, computed). Wrap kNN in a Pipeline([StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(...)]). (bincount in the from-scratch version also assumes non-negative integer labels — fine for iris targets {0,1,2}, but it would break on string labels.)

4. Worked examples

Example 1 — iris, from scratch vs sklearn (the cross-check)

The course’s anchor example loads iris, splits 80/20, and runs kNN both ways on the same split so the two implementations can be checked against each other (notebook 03_Supervised_learning_with_kNN (1).ipynb, cells 4, 8, 12):

from sklearn import datasets
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

iris = datasets.load_iris()
X, y = iris.data, iris.target                       # 150 × 4 features, 3 species
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    X, y, test_size=0.2, random_state=10)          # 120 train, 30 test

With K = 3 on this fixed split (random_state=10), the from-scratch classifier and KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=3) both score 96.67% on the 30-point test set (29/30 correct) — they agree exactly, which is the point of the cross-check (computed; the notebook stores no outputs for cells 8/12). The single test point inspected first (cell 7) has true label 1 and is predicted 1 (computed). Iris is nearly linearly separable, so even this minimal classifier with no scaling does well.

Example 2 — choosing K is choosing your place on the bias–variance dial

Sweeping K on the same iris split makes the bias–variance trade-off concrete (computed, random_state=10):

K train accuracy test accuracy
1 100.00% 96.67%
3 96.67% 96.67%
11 97.50% 100.00%
50 93.33% 93.33%
100 75.83% 60.00%

At K = 1 the training accuracy is a perfect 100% — every point is its own nearest neighbor — the textbook signature of high variance (it has memorized the training set). As K grows, the vote averages over a wider neighborhood; by K = 100 (out of only 120 training points) the model is essentially predicting the global majority class and collapses to 60% on test — textbook high bias / underfit (Lecture 2, slide 8/10). The sweet spot is in between, and you don’t read it off the test set — you pick it by cross-validation on the training data (5-fold CV here is flat at ≈ 94% for K = 3…25, computed; iris is too easy to produce a sharp optimum). The principle generalizes: small K overfits, large K underfits, choose K by CV (Lecture 2, slide 8/10).

Example 3 — why scaling decides the outcome (wine)

To show the scaling pitfall from §3 with numbers, take wine (13 chemical features on very different scales) with a 75/25 split (random_state=1), K = 3 (computed):

from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline

raw    = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=3).fit(Xtr, ytr).score(Xte, yte)        # 0.6889
scaled = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=3)
                       ).fit(Xtr, ytr).score(Xte, yte)                            # 0.9778

Raw-feature kNN gets 68.89%; standardizing first lifts it to 97.78% — a 29-point jump from one preprocessing line. The reason is mechanical: proline ranges in the hundreds-to-thousands (std ≈ 314) while other features sit near std ≈ 0.12, so before scaling the Euclidean distance is almost entirely the proline difference and the other twelve features barely vote. This is exactly why the Spring-2025 exam’s Assignment 4 explicitly asks you to “scale if necessary” and notes that “distance/gradient models like k-NN” need it, while trees do not (§7).

This is the picture that earns its place for kNN: it makes the algorithm itself visible. The dashed segments are the very distances kNN computes and ranks; the three ringed points are the vote; the star takes their majority color. It also shows why K matters — drop the test point near a blob boundary and the K = 3 ring straddles two colors, the regime where small K is jumpy (high variance) and a larger K would steady the vote. This mirrors the notebook’s own visualization idiom (a starred test point with its nearest neighbors highlighted on make_blobs data, cell 10), rebuilt with deterministic in-browser data so it needs no network or stored dataset.

6. Diagnostics & interpretation

What to look at to decide whether a kNN model is working:

  • Accuracy on a held-out set, and beyond accuracy — report precision, recall, F1 when classes are imbalanced (the Spring-2025 exam insists on this for Assignment 4, §7). A confusion matrix shows which classes the neighbors confuse.
  • The K curve (train vs CV accuracy across K) — the diagnostic that locates your bias–variance position (Example 2). A huge train–CV gap at small K signals variance; both sagging at large K signals bias. Pick K at the CV peak, never on the test set.
  • Did you scale? — the first thing to check on any disappointing kNN result. An unscaled distance-based model is the most common silent failure (Example 3): inspect feature ranges/standard deviations.
  • Dimensionality — as features multiply, “nearest” loses meaning (the curse of dimensionality, §8 Mode B). If accuracy degrades as you add features, that is the symptom; reduce with PCA or switch to a feature-selecting model.
  • Predict-time cost — there are no coefficients to read; a kNN “explanation” is the neighbor set itself. Watch query latency on large training sets (Lecture 2, slide 8/10).

Comparative diagnostic vs a sibling: logistic regression hands you coefficients — a single global, interpretable boundary you can write down and reason about (“the odds rise with X”). kNN hands you a local vote — no global rule, but a boundary that bends to the data wherever it needs to. This is the parametric-vs-non-parametric trade (Lecture 2, slide 9/10): the logistic model generalizes from a fixed form and few parameters; kNN’s complexity grows with the data and lives entirely in K and the metric. Match the choice to the question — interpretable global effect, or flexible local fit.

7. Past-exam lens

How kNN shows up. In the Spring-2025 take-home, Assignment 4 (“Predictive classification challenge” on SimulatedData.csv, exercises 4.1–4.9, phase0/exam_spring2025_notebook.md), kNN is named as a candidate classifier. Two exercises bear on it directly: 4.3 asks you to “encode categoricals, scale if necessary” and explicitly notes that “trees may not need scaling; distance/gradient models like k-NN or logistic regression do” — so if you submit kNN, the scaling decision from §3/§4 is being graded. 4.4 asks for at least two classifiers (a simple baseline plus a more complex model) with justification, 4.5 for hyperparameter tuning via CV (for kNN that is choosing K, §2/§4), and 4.6 for accuracy/precision/recall/F1. Crucially, 4.8 is run by the grader on a hidden hold-out test set, so your preprocessing (the scaler!) must be wired into a pipeline that generalizes to unseen rows — a raw, unscaled kNN that “worked” on your split will misfire on theirs. The data is simulated with “non-linear effects and noise,” which is the regime where a non-parametric method like kNN can shine — or, if you skip scaling and ignore the curse of dimensionality, quietly fail. kNN has not appeared as a stand-alone derivation question; it appears as a baseline classifier to deploy and justify.

8. Math

Math is off. Use the Math toggle in the top bar to show the course-level derivation (and “full” for the rigorous version).

Mode A — course level. (Throughout, K is the number of neighbors; the course/sklearn write this as k/n_neighbors, and p is the number of features — see the notation table.) kNN has nothing to fit; the whole method is a distance and a vote. The course defines distance as the Euclidean metric (notebook 03_Supervised_learning_with_kNN (1).ipynb, cell 3; Lecture 2, slide 8/10 names Euclidean as the metric “that matters”) — between a query x and a training point x' in p features, d(x,x') = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\bigl(x_j - x'_j\bigr)^2}, which is exactly what the from-scratch code computes, np.sqrt(np.sum((x - y)**2)) (cell 7). Because every coordinate enters in its raw units, a feature on a larger numeric scale dominates the sum — the algebraic reason scaling is mandatory (§3, Example 3).

To predict, take the set \mathcal{N}_K(x) of the K training points with the smallest d(x,x') (the code’s np.argsort(distances)[:K]) and vote. For classification with classes c, assign the majority label, \hat{y}(x) = \arg\max_{c}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_K(x)} \mathbb{1}[\,y_i = c\,], which the notebook implements as np.bincount(...).argmax() (cell 7). For regression the same neighborhood is averaged, \hat{y}(x) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_K(x)} y_i (Lecture 2, slide 8/10 — kNN does “classification (label) + regression (value)”). The sklearn default weights='uniform' gives every neighbor an equal vote; the weighted-vote variant weights='distance' weights neighbor i by 1/d(x,x_i), letting closer points count more (§3).

Reading K as the bias–variance dial. K=1 copies the nearest label, so the boundary chases individual points — high variance; large K averages over a wide neighborhood and washes out local structure toward the global majority — high bias (Lecture 2, slide 8/10). The course’s prescription is therefore to choose K by cross-validation, never off the test set (Lecture 2, slide 8/10; Example 2). The rigorous version of “why distances stop being informative as p grows,” and the asymptotic optimality that justifies kNN at all, are in Mode B. Foundations: linear-algebra essentials.

Mode B — full rigor. (Supplemental — the course slides treat kNN conceptually only, Lecture 2 slide 8/10. Anchored to ESL §13.3 “k-Nearest-Neighbor Classifiers” (printed pp. 463–468) and ESL §2.5 “Local Methods in High Dimensions” (printed pp. 22–25), with ISL §2.2.3 for the Bayes connection.)

The Bayes target. kNN is best understood as an estimator of the Bayes classifier, the rule that minimizes test error by assigning x_0 to the class j maximizing \Pr(Y=j \mid X=x_0) (ISL eq. 2.10). Its error, the irreducible Bayes error rate 1 - \mathrm{E}\!\bigl[\max_j \Pr(Y=j\mid X)\bigr] (ISL eq. 2.11), is the floor no classifier can beat. Since the conditional distribution is unknown, kNN estimates it by the neighborhood frequency, \Pr(Y=j \mid X=x_0) \approx \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}_0} \mathbb{1}(y_i = j) \qquad \text{(ISL eq. 2.12),} then classifies to the largest. ESL writes the same memory-based rule with Euclidean distance d_{(i)} = \lVert x_{(i)} - x_0 \rVert (ESL eq. 13.1), noting one “first standardize[s] each of the features to have mean zero and variance 1” (ESL §13.3) — the rigorous statement of §3’s scaling rule.

1-NN error ≤ 2× Bayes (Cover–Hart). ESL gives the classic asymptotic bound. Let k^\ast be the dominant class at x and p_k(x) the true conditional probability of class k. Then the Bayes error is 1 - p_{k^\ast}(x) (ESL eq. 13.2), while the asymptotic 1-nearest-neighbor error is the probability the query and its (asymptotically coincident) neighbor disagree: \text{1-NN error} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k(x)\,\bigl(1 - p_k(x)\bigr) \;\ge\; 1 - p_{k^\ast}(x) \qquad \text{(ESL eqs. 13.3–13.4).} In the two-class case this is 2\,p_{k^\ast}(x)\bigl(1-p_{k^\ast}(x)\bigr) \le 2\bigl(1-p_{k^\ast}(x)\bigr) — at most twice the Bayes rate (Cover & Hart, 1967; ESL §13.3). The general-K refinement is \sum_k p_k(1-p_k) \le 2(1-p_{k^\ast}) - \tfrac{K}{K-1}(1-p_{k^\ast})^2 (ESL eq. 13.5). The catch is the “kicker” ESL flags: this assumes the bias is zero — true only asymptotically, when the data fill the space densely enough that the nearest neighbor coincides with the query. That assumption is exactly what the curse of dimensionality destroys.

Curse of dimensionality. In high p, neighbors are not near. ESL §2.5 makes this precise: to capture a fraction r of uniformly-distributed data with a hypercubical neighborhood, the expected edge length is e_p(r) = r^{1/p}, so in p=10 dimensions, capturing just 1% of the data needs e_{10}(0.01) = 0.63 and 10% needs e_{10}(0.1) = 0.80 of the entire range of every feature (ESL §2.5, Fig. 2.6) — the “neighborhood” is no longer local. Equivalently, the median distance from the origin to its nearest of N points is d(p,N) = \bigl(1 - \tfrac{1}{2}^{1/N}\bigr)^{1/p} (ESL eq. 2.24), which grows toward the boundary as p rises, and the sampling density scales as N^{1/p}, so matching a one-dimensional density of N_1=100 at p=10 would need 100^{10} points (ESL §2.5). ISL §3.5 confirms the practical consequence: as noise predictors are added (Fig. 3.20), KNN’s test MSE degrades far faster than a parametric model’s, because “spreading [the] observations over p … dimensions results in a phenomenon in which a given observation has no nearby neighbors” (ISL §3.5, curse of dimensionality). This is why §2/§6 push dimension reduction (PCA) or a feature-selecting model before kNN in wide data — and why K must be tuned by cross-validation (ESL Fig. 13.4 plots exactly this train/test/10-fold-CV-vs-K trade-off).
