How to read this appendix. Each foundation below has a Mode A — course level block (what the course teaches and grades) and a Mode B — full rigor block (complete derivations, anchored to ESL). Use the top-bar Math toggle to reveal them. Method pages link into the relevant section. Two foundations — bias–variance and information criteria — have full method pages of their own, so this appendix states the key result and links out to them; the other five are derived here.

This appendix collects the shared mathematical machinery the method pages depend on, so each method page can link here instead of re-deriving foundations.

Linear algebra essentials

Mode A — course level. The course assumes this layer rather than teaching it: Lecture 1 (slide 16/17) names “the math” as a prerequisite you must be able to justify, and from Lecture 2 onward every linear model is written directly in matrix form. So the working vocabulary is taken as given:

  • Vectors and matrices. A feature vector is x=(x_1,\dots,x_p)^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{p}; stacking n of them as rows gives the design matrix X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p} (a leading column of ones absorbs the intercept), and the target is the n-vector y (notebook 02_OLS (1).ipynb, cell 7; Lecture 2, slide 7/10).
  • Inner product and norm. The inner product a^\top b=\sum_j a_j b_j measures alignment; the squared Euclidean (L2) norm is \lVert a\rVert_2^2 = a^\top a = \sum_j a_j^2. Squared error is just a squared norm: \mathrm{RSS}(\beta)=\lVert y - X\beta\rVert_2^2. The course also uses the L1 norm \lVert\beta\rVert_1=\sum_j|\beta_j| (Lasso) and the squared-L2 \lVert\beta\rVert_2^2 (Ridge) as penalties (Lecture 3, slides 14–15).
  • Projection. Fitting OLS means projecting y onto the span of the columns of X: \hat y = X\hat\beta is the point in that subspace closest to y, and the residual y-\hat y is orthogonal to it. The closed form is the normal-equations solution \hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y (notebook 02_OLS (1).ipynb, cell 8).

This is enough to read every linear-model page. The eigen/SVD machinery the course leans on implicitly — for PCA (which the slides give only conceptually, [R9-5]) and for understanding Ridge — is supplied in Mode B from ESL.

Mode B — full rigor. (Anchored to ESL §3.4.1, eqs 3.45–3.49, PDF p84–85; the OLS projection geometry is ESL §3.2, eqs 3.6–3.7.)

Rank, column space, projection. For X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p} the column space \mathrm{col}(X) is the subspace of \mathbb{R}^{n} spanned by its columns; X has full column rank when those columns are linearly independent. The OLS fit is the orthogonal projection of y onto \mathrm{col}(X): \hat y = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y = Hy with the symmetric idempotent hat matrix H=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top (H^2=H, ESL 3.7). When X is rank-deficient X^\top X is singular and \hat\beta is not unique (though \hat y still is) — the failure mode that motivates Ridge.

Eigendecomposition and the spectral theorem. Any symmetric matrix S=S^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p} admits a real eigendecomposition S = V\Lambda V^\top with V orthogonal (V^\top V=I, columns the eigenvectors) and \Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_p) the real eigenvalues — the spectral theorem. S is positive semidefinite (S\succeq 0, i.e. a^\top S a\ge 0 for all a) iff all \lambda_j\ge 0, and positive definite (S\succ 0) iff all \lambda_j>0. The Gram matrix X^\top X is always PSD, and PD exactly when X has full column rank — which is why (X^\top X)^{-1} exists precisely then, and why the OLS Hessian 2X^\top X\succeq 0 makes \mathrm{RSS} convex (cf. OLS §8).

Singular value decomposition. The SVD of the (centered) N\times p matrix X is X = UDV^\top \qquad \text{(ESL 3.45),} where U (N\times p) and V (p\times p) have orthonormal columns and D=\mathrm{diag}(d_1\ge\cdots\ge d_p\ge 0) holds the singular values; d_j=0 signals singularity. Two roles make the SVD the workhorse of this course:

  1. Least squares. Substituting the SVD, the fitted vector is X\hat\beta^{\mathrm{ls}} = UU^\top y (ESL 3.46) — the projection of y onto the orthonormal basis U for \mathrm{col}(X). Ridge instead computes X\hat\beta^{\mathrm{ridge}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}u_j\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}u_j^\top y (ESL 3.47): it keeps the same basis but shrinks each coordinate by d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda)\le 1, shrinking the directions of small d_j (near-collinear directions) the most.
  2. PCA. Since X^\top X = VD^2V^\top (ESL 3.48) is the eigendecomposition of (a multiple of) the sample covariance S=X^\top X/N, the columns v_j of V are the principal-component directions and the derived variables z_j=Xv_j=u_jd_j are the principal components, with \mathrm{Var}(z_1)=d_1^2/N the largest possible (ESL 3.49). This is the eigen/SVD foundation the PCA page builds on (its §8 links here), supplied because the course slides give PCA conceptually only [R9-5].

Gradient descent

Mode A — course level. Gradient descent is the course’s general-purpose optimizer, introduced from first principles in Lecture 8 (slides 3–5/29). It minimizes a loss L(\theta) by repeatedly stepping opposite the gradient — the direction of steepest descent: \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta L, where the gradient \nabla_\theta L=(\partial L/\partial\theta_1,\dots)^\top points in the direction of steepest ascent (slide 4/29), so the minus sign turns it into descent, and the learning rate \eta>0 (step size) scales how far each step moves (slide 3/29). Too small \eta ⇒ slow convergence; too large ⇒ overshooting / divergence. The three variants differ only in how much data is used per gradient estimate (slide 5/29):

  • Batch GD — average the gradient over the entire training set before each update: stable, but one slow update per epoch.
  • Stochastic GD (SGD) — update from a single example at a time: fast and noisy.
  • Mini-batch GD — gradient over a small batch (the course’s practical default, e.g. 32 or 64): a compromise that is vectorizable and more stable than pure SGD.

This same update drives logistic regression, ADALINE, and neural-network training (NN basics, build a NN) — in the NN setting the per-parameter gradients come from backpropagation. (Notation: Lecture 8 also writes the learning rate as \alpha in its generic backprop section; this site standardizes on \eta — see the notation table.) The course does not treat convergence theory or step-size conditions formally; those are in Mode B.

Mode B — full rigor. (Anchored to ESL §11.4, PDF p414–417: GD update eq 11.13, and the step-size / stochastic-approximation conditions on p417.) ESL presents gradient descent as the engine for fitting neural networks. With a sum-of-squares loss R(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}R_i, the batch update at iteration r{+}1 is \theta^{(r+1)} = \theta^{(r)} - \gamma_r\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial R_i}{\partial \theta}\Big|_{\theta^{(r)}} \qquad \text{(ESL 11.13),} with \gamma_r the learning rate (\eta in the course’s notation).

Batch vs online (SGD). “The updates in (11.13) are a kind of batch learning, with the parameter updates being a sum over all of the training cases” (ESL §11.4); online / stochastic learning replaces that sum with “a single summand” — one training case at a time, cycling through the data over many epochs — which “allows the network to handle very large training sets” (ESL §11.4, p417). Mini-batch is the intermediate case.

Step-size conditions and convergence. For batch learning ESL notes the rate “is usually taken to be a constant, and can also be optimized by a line search that minimizes the error function at each update.” For online learning \gamma_r “should decrease to zero as the iteration r\to\infty”; this is stochastic approximation (Robbins & Munro, 1951), whose convergence is guaranteed when \gamma_r\to 0,\qquad \textstyle\sum_r \gamma_r=\infty,\qquad \sum_r \gamma_r^2<\infty “(satisfied, for example, by \gamma_r=1/r)” (ESL §11.4, p417). The first divergent-sum condition lets the iterates travel any finite distance; the second square-summable condition damps the gradient noise to zero so the iterates settle.

Convexity, the quadratic case, and momentum. When R is convex every stationary point is a global minimum, so a small enough constant step converges; the OLS objective is the canonical convex (in fact quadratic) example — its Hessian 2X^\top X\succeq 0 (§sec-linalg; OLS §8), and on a quadratic the convergence rate is governed by the condition number (ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalue d_1^2/d_p^2 of X^\top X): ill-conditioned (near-collinear) problems force a small \eta and crawl, the same small-d_j directions the SVD flags. Neural-net objectives are non-convex, so GD finds only local minima and “back-propagation can be very slow” (ESL §11.4, p417); ESL recommends conjugate gradients and variable-metric (quasi-Newton) methods as faster alternatives that avoid forming the Hessian. Momentum — accumulating a running average of past gradients to accelerate along consistent directions and damp oscillation across a narrow valley — is the standard practical fix for this ill-conditioning (a deep-learning staple beyond the course/ESL §11.4 scope, noted here for completeness).

Maximum likelihood & MAP

Mode A — course level. The likelihood \mathcal{L}(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}p(y_i\mid x_i;\theta) is the probability the model assigns to the observed data as a function of the parameters; maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE) picks the \theta that maximizes it. Because products are awkward, one maximizes the log-likelihood \ell(\theta)=\sum_i\log p(y_i\mid x_i;\theta) instead (same maximizer, sums not products). The course uses MLE as the unifying justification for two of its core models:

  • Least squares = MLE under Gaussian noise. If y_i=\beta^\top x_i+\varepsilon_i with \varepsilon_i\sim N(0,\sigma^2), maximizing the log-likelihood is identical to minimizing the residual sum of squares — so OLS is the MLE (Lecture 2, slide 7/10; OLS §8 Mode A).
  • Logistic regression = MLE under Bernoulli. Modelling y_i\in\{0,1\} as Bernoulli with \pi_i=\sigma(\beta^\top x_i), the log-likelihood is the negative cross-entropy, so “maximize likelihood ≡ minimize cross-entropy loss” (Lecture 8, slide 25/29; Lecture 4, slide 6/11 — “often fit by maximum likelihood”). There is no closed form, so it is fit by gradient descent / Newton’s method.

The course treats MLE concretely (these two cases) and does not cover the exponential family or the MAP/penalized-MLE bridge formally — those are supplied in Mode B from ESL, where they explain why Ridge and Lasso penalties have the form they do.

Mode B — full rigor. (Anchored to ESL §4.4 logistic MLE, eqs 4.19–4.21, PDF p139–140; and ESL §3.4.1 priors / MAP, the posterior-mode result on PDF p84.)

Logistic MLE in full. Coding the two-class response as y_i\in\{0,1\} with p(x_i;\beta)=\Pr(y_i{=}1\mid x_i), the log-likelihood (ESL 4.19–4.20) is \ell(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl\{y_i\log p(x_i;\beta)+(1-y_i)\log\bigl(1-p(x_i;\beta)\bigr)\Bigr\}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl\{y_i\beta^\top x_i-\log\bigl(1+e^{\beta^\top x_i}\bigr)\Bigr\}. Setting the gradient to zero gives the score equations \partial\ell/\partial\beta=\sum_i x_i\bigl(y_i-p(x_i;\beta)\bigr)=0 (ESL 4.21) — nonlinear in \beta, solved by Newton–Raphson, which ESL shows is iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS), \beta^{\mathrm{new}}=(X^\top WX)^{-1}X^\top Wz (ESL 4.26). Note the score has the same form \sum_i x_i(y_i-\text{prediction}) as the course’s gradient \partial L/\partial w_j=-\tfrac1n\sum_i(y_i-\sigma(z_i))x_{ij} (Lecture 8, slide 28) — the link the course makes between ADALINE and logistic GD [R9-11].

Exponential family. Both Gaussian and Bernoulli belong to the exponential family p(y\mid\eta)=h(y)\exp\!\bigl(\eta^\top T(y)-A(\eta)\bigr), whose log-likelihood is concave in the natural parameter \eta — the structural reason the logistic log-likelihood is concave and the MLE unique (ESL §4.4 notes “the log-likelihood is concave”). Generalized linear models set \eta=\beta^\top x, recovering linear and logistic regression as special cases.

MAP = penalized MLE. Place a prior p(\beta) on the parameters; the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate maximizes the posterior \log p(\beta\mid y)=\ell(\beta)+\log p(\beta)+\text{const} — i.e. log-likelihood plus a log-prior penalty. ESL makes this explicit for Ridge: “Ridge regression can also be derived as the mean or mode of a posterior distribution… suppose y_i\sim N(\beta_0+x_i^\top\beta,\sigma^2) and the parameters \beta_j are each distributed as N(0,\tau^2)… then the (negative) log-posterior density of \beta is equal to the [penalized RSS], with \lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2. Thus the ridge estimate is the mode of the posterior distribution” (ESL §3.4.1, PDF p84). Hence: \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_i(y_i-\beta^\top x_i)^2 + \lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2}_{\text{Ridge}}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\text{Gaussian prior }N(0,\tau^2),\qquad \underbrace{\cdots + \lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_1}_{\text{Lasso}}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\text{Laplace (double-exponential) prior.} The L2 penalty is the log of a Gaussian prior (\log e^{-\beta^2/2\tau^2}\propto-\beta^2); the L1 penalty is the log of a Laplace prior (\log e^{-|\beta|/b}\propto-|\beta|), whose sharp peak at zero is what produces Lasso’s exact zeros — see KKT §sec-kkt and the regularization page. This same likelihood-with-parameter-penalty structure underlies AIC/BIC (§sec-infocrit), which trade fit (-2\ln\mathcal{L}) against a parameter count.

Bias–variance decomposition

This foundation has a full method page. The bias–variance trade-off is derived in detail on the Bias–Variance page — see its §8 Math for the complete derivation (the add-and-subtract expansion, the kNN and linear-model special cases). This section states only the key result and points there.

Mode A — course level. The course states the trade-off as a two-term decomposition (Lecture 5, slide 10/11): \text{Total Error} = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance}. Bias is systematic error from over-simple assumptions (underfitting); variance is sensitivity to the particular training set (overfitting). More complexity lowers bias but raises variance; the goal is the sum-minimizing sweet spot. Full treatment and the polynomial-degree demo: Bias–Variance §8 Mode A.

Mode B — full rigor. (ESL §7.3, eq 7.9.) The full expected prediction error at a point x_0 under squared-error loss adds the irreducible error \sigma^2 the course slide drops [R9-2]: \mathbb{E}\bigl[(y-\hat f(x_0))^2\bigr]=\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}}+\underbrace{\bigl[\mathbb{E}\hat f(x_0)-f(x_0)\bigr]^2}_{\text{Bias}^2}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat f(x_0)-\mathbb{E}\hat f(x_0)\bigr]^2}_{\text{Var}}\qquad\text{(ESL 7.9).} \sigma^2 is the noise floor no model can beat. The derivation, plus the kNN form (ESL 7.10) and the linear-model \tfrac{p}{N}\sigma_\varepsilon^2 variance term (ESL 7.12), is the primary home: Bias–Variance §8 Mode B.

Information criteria

This foundation has a full method page. Information criteria and cross-validation are derived in detail on the CV & Information Criteria page — see its §8 Math for the AIC optimism/Kullback–Leibler argument, the BIC Laplace approximation, the ISL linear-model forms, and the CV equations. This section states only the key results and points there.

Mode A — course level. Both information criteria score a model by its maximized likelihood \mathcal{L} minus a penalty for its number of parameters d (Lecture 5, slide 6/11; the slide writes d as k and the likelihood as L — relabelled per the notation table to free k for folds): \mathrm{AIC}=2d-2\ln(\mathcal{L}),\qquad \mathrm{BIC}=\ln(n)\,d-2\ln(\mathcal{L}). Same fit term -2\ln(\mathcal{L}); BIC’s per-parameter price \ln(n) exceeds AIC’s 2 once n>e^2\approx 7.4, so BIC favours simpler models. Lower is better, comparable only on the same data. k-fold cross-validation is the model-agnostic alternative: split into V folds, train on V{-}1, score on the held-out one, rotate, and average. Full treatment: CV & Info Criteria §8 Mode A.

Mode B — full rigor. (ESL §7.5 (AIC), §7.7 (BIC), §7.10 (CV); ISL §6.1.3 linear-model forms.) AIC is the optimism / Kullback–Leibler bias correction \mathrm{AIC}=-\tfrac{2}{N}\,\mathrm{loglik}+2\tfrac{d}{N} (ESL 7.29–7.30); BIC is the Schwarz criterion -2\,\mathrm{loglik}+(\log N)d (ESL 7.35) derived from a Laplace approximation to the marginal likelihood (ESL 7.40). ISL’s linear-model forms differ only by scaling/constants and select the same model [R9-3]. The CV estimate is \mathrm{CV}(\hat f)=\tfrac1N\sum_i L(y_i,\hat f^{-\kappa(i)}(x_i)) (ESL 7.48), with the choice of fold count K itself a bias–variance trade-off of the estimator. The complete derivations are the primary home: CV & Info Criteria §8 Mode B.

Backpropagation

Mode A — course level. The course derives backpropagation for a single-hidden-layer network from the chain rule, link by link (Lecture 8, slides 11–12; Lecture 9, slides 13–14). For a network f(x)=\beta_0+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_k\,A_k with hidden activations A_{ik}=g(z_{ik}) and pre-activations z_{ik}=w_{k0}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_{kj}x_{ij}, and squared-error loss L_i=\tfrac12(y_i-f_i)^2:

1. Forward pass — compute and store z_{ik}, A_{ik}, and the output f_i for each observation (Lecture 9, slide 13/17).

2. Backward pass (chain rule) — starting from the loss and working back (Lecture 9, slide 14/17): \frac{\partial L_i}{\partial f_i}=-(y_i-f_i),\qquad \boxed{\frac{\partial L_i}{\partial \beta_k}=-(y_i-f_i)\,A_{ik}},\qquad \boxed{\frac{\partial L_i}{\partial w_{kj}}=-(y_i-f_i)\,\beta_k\,g'(z_{ik})\,x_{ij}}. The output-weight gradient has two chained links (\partial L_i/\partial f_i then \partial f_i/\partial\beta_k=A_{ik}); the hidden-weight gradient has four (-(y_i-f_i), \beta_k, g'(z_{ik}), x_{ij}). “Each parameter receives a fraction of the residual (y_i-f_i) propagated back — hence backpropagation” (Lecture 9, slide 14/17).

3. Update by gradient descent: \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\nabla_\theta L (Lecture 8, slide 13/29), summing gradients over a batch / mini-batch / single example. This is the exact derivation reused on the neural-network basics, autodiff, PyTorch, and build-a-NN pages. Mode B gives the general-depth \delta-recursion and the reverse-mode AD view.

Mode B — full rigor. (Anchored to ESL §11.3–11.4, PDF p411–417: model eqs 11.5–11.6, loss 11.9, derivatives 11.12, GD update 11.13, \delta-recursion 11.14–11.15; weight decay §11.5.2, eq 11.16.)

The model and loss. ESL’s single-hidden-layer network is Z_m=\sigma(\alpha_{0m}+\alpha_m^\top X), T_k=\beta_{0k}+\beta_k^\top Z, f_k(X)=g_k(T) (ESL 11.5), with sigmoid \sigma(v)=1/(1+e^{-v}) and, for K-class output, softmax g_k(T)=e^{T_k}/\sum_\ell e^{T_\ell} (ESL 11.6). Regression uses sum-of-squares R(\theta)=\sum_{k}\sum_{i}(y_{ik}-f_k(x_i))^2 (ESL 11.9); classification uses cross-entropy/deviance (ESL 11.10).

The two-pass derivatives. Writing z_{mi}=\sigma(\alpha_{0m}+\alpha_m^\top x_i), ESL gives (eq 11.12) \frac{\partial R_i}{\partial \beta_{km}}=-2(y_{ik}-f_k(x_i))\,g_k'(\beta_k^\top z_i)\,z_{mi},\qquad \frac{\partial R_i}{\partial \alpha_{m\ell}}=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}2(y_{ik}-f_k(x_i))\,g_k'(\beta_k^\top z_i)\,\beta_{km}\,\sigma'(\alpha_m^\top x_i)\,x_{i\ell}, and the gradient-descent update \beta_{km}^{(r+1)}=\beta_{km}^{(r)}-\gamma_r\sum_i\partial R_i/\partial\beta_{km}^{(r)}, likewise for \alpha (ESL 11.13). These match the course’s boxed Mode-A gradients exactly (the course takes K{=}1, g_k= identity on the output, and drops the factor 2 into the loss).

The \delta-recursion. Define the error signals by rewriting (11.12) as \partial R_i/\partial\beta_{km}=\delta_{ki}z_{mi} and \partial R_i/\partial\alpha_{m\ell}=s_{mi}x_{i\ell} (ESL 11.14). Then the hidden-layer errors satisfy the back-propagation equation s_{mi}=\sigma'(\alpha_m^\top x_i)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{km}\,\delta_{ki}\qquad\text{(ESL 11.15),} expressing each hidden unit’s error as the \sigma'-weighted sum of the output errors it feeds — the rule that generalizes to arbitrary depth: errors at layer \ell are computed from errors at layer \ell{+}1 via the transpose of that layer’s weights, scaled by the local activation derivative. ESL’s two-pass algorithm (p416): a forward pass fixes the weights and computes all \hat f_k(x_i) from (11.5); a backward pass computes the output errors \delta_{ki}, back-propagates them via (11.15) to the hidden errors s_{mi}, and both sets give the gradients via (11.14). Mapping to the course’s notation, ESL’s \delta_{ki}\equiv -(y_i-f_i) at the output (with identity g) and s_{mi}\equiv -(y_i-f_i)\beta_k\sigma'(z_k) at the hidden layer [R9-10].

Matrix form and reverse-mode AD. With layers as matrix multiplies A=g(\mathbf 1_n w_0^\top+XW^\top), f=\beta_0\mathbf 1_n+A\beta (Lecture 9, slide 7/17), the forward pass is a chain of matmul-then-activation and the backward pass propagates a gradient matrix back through the transposed weights — exactly reverse-mode automatic differentiation: backprop is reverse-mode AD applied to the network’s computational graph, traversing it once forward to cache intermediates, once backward to accumulate \partial L/\partial(\cdot) via the chain rule (the view the course’s autodiff page and live-coded engine make concrete; Lecture 8, slides 8–10). “The advantages of back-propagation are its simple, local nature” — each unit exchanges information only with units it connects to (ESL §11.4, p417). ESL also calls it the delta rule (Widrow–Hoff, 1960).

Weight decay (regularization). To avoid overfitting the global minimizer of R, ESL adds a penalty R(\theta)+\lambda J(\theta) with J(\theta)=\sum_{km}\beta_{km}^2+\sum_{m\ell}\alpha_{m\ell}^2 (ESL 11.16), “analogous to ridge regression”; its only effect on backprop is to add 2\beta_{km} and 2\alpha_{m\ell} to the respective gradients. (The course’s Lecture 9 slide 16 penalizes only the hidden weights w_{kj}, whereas ESL — and the course’s own notebook 19 — penalize all weights [R9-9].)

KKT conditions

Mode A — course level. Constrained optimization and the KKT conditions are not covered in the course lectures. What the course does show is the geometric picture it sits behind: the Ridge (circular L2) and Lasso (diamond L1) constraint regions, with Lasso’s corners forcing coefficients to exactly zero — and the slide explicitly attributes that figure to “Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman (2009),” i.e. ESL (Lecture 3, slide 16/20). The course states the constrained↔︎penalized link only informally (\lambda=0\Rightarrow OLS, \lambda\to\infty\Rightarrow all coefficients \to 0; Lecture 3, slide 19/20). For the actual constrained-optimization machinery — Lagrangian duality, the KKT conditions, and why the diamond’s corners produce exact zeros — see Mode B, supplied from ESL §3.4.

Mode B — full rigor. (Anchored to ESL §3.4, PDF p83–90: the constrained/penalized ridge forms eqs 3.41–3.44, the lasso constrained form, and the orthonormal soft-threshold solution Table 3.4 / Fig 3.11.)

The constrained ⇔ penalized equivalence. ESL writes Ridge in both a penalized (Lagrangian) form and a constrained form: \hat\beta^{\mathrm{ridge}}=\arg\min_\beta\Bigl\{\textstyle\sum_i(y_i-\beta_0-\sum_j x_{ij}\beta_j)^2+\lambda\sum_j\beta_j^2\Bigr\}\ \text{(ESL 3.41)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \arg\min_\beta\textstyle\sum_i(\cdots)^2\ \text{s.t. }\sum_j\beta_j^2\le t\ \text{(ESL 3.42),} and states there is “a one-to-one correspondence between the parameters \lambda in (3.41) and t in (3.42).” This is Lagrangian duality: the penalized objective is the Lagrangian of the constrained problem, with \lambda\ge 0 the Lagrange multiplier on the constraint \sum_j\beta_j^2\le t. The same holds for Lasso with the constraint \sum_j|\beta_j|\le t (\lVert\beta\rVert_q\le t in general). Larger \lambda ⇔ smaller budget t.

The KKT conditions. For \min_\beta R(\beta) subject to c(\beta)=\lVert\beta\rVert_q-t\le 0, the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions characterize the optimum (\beta^\star,\lambda^\star): \nabla R(\beta^\star)+\lambda^\star\nabla c(\beta^\star)=0\ \text{(stationarity)},\quad c(\beta^\star)\le 0\ \text{(feasibility)},\quad \lambda^\star\ge 0\ \text{(dual feasibility)},\quad \lambda^\star\,c(\beta^\star)=0\ \text{(complementary slackness)}. Complementary slackness is the load-bearing one: either the constraint is slack (\lVert\beta^\star\rVert_q<t) and then \lambda^\star=0 (the OLS solution, if it already satisfies the budget), or the constraint binds (\lVert\beta^\star\rVert_q=t) and \lambda^\star>0.

Why Lasso gives exact zeros. For an orthonormal design (X^\top X=I) the KKT conditions solve in closed form, and ESL’s Table 3.4 (PDF p90) gives the three estimators explicitly: \hat\beta_j^{\mathrm{ridge}}=\frac{\hat\beta_j}{1+\lambda},\qquad \hat\beta_j^{\mathrm{lasso}}=\operatorname{sign}(\hat\beta_j)\bigl(|\hat\beta_j|-\lambda\bigr)_+\ \text{(soft-thresholding)}, where \hat\beta_j is the OLS estimate and (\cdot)_+ the positive part. Ridge’s penalty is smooth (\nabla\sum\beta_j^2=2\beta), so its stationarity equation only scales coefficients — they shrink but stay nonzero. Lasso’s L1 penalty is non-differentiable at \beta_j=0; its KKT stationarity uses the subgradient of |\beta_j| (the interval [-1,1] at zero), and whenever the unpenalized gradient |\hat\beta_j|\le\lambda falls inside that interval, \beta_j=0 satisfies the optimality conditions exactly — the coefficient is set to zero, not merely shrunk. Geometrically (ESL Fig 3.11, PDF p90), the elliptical RSS contours first touch the L1 diamond at a corner (where some coordinates vanish), whereas they touch the L2 ball generically on its smooth surface (no zeros). This is the exact-sparsity mechanism the regularization page relies on (its §8 links here), and it is the optimization-side counterpart of the Laplace-prior MAP view in §sec-mle.
