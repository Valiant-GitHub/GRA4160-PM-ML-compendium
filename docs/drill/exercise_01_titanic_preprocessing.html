Drill: Titanic Preprocessing

Foundations / preprocessing drill · The one exercise that is pure data preparation — no model is fitted. Load the raw Titanic table, fix missing values, tame outliers, encode the categoricals, and read survival straight off the data as conditional probabilities.

1. What this exercise teaches

Hands-on preprocessing and EDA on a real, messy tabular dataset: load and inspect, impute missing values, treat outliers (focus on Fare), encode Sex and Embarked, and compute survival probabilities by group. It is the drill that builds the habits every later modelling exercise assumes — the data-cleaning that happens before any estimator.

This is the only no-model exercise. Every other drill starts from cleaned data or a sklearn toy dataset. Here the whole task is the cleaning. Treat it as the canonical worked example of Pattern F — Missing data handling and the diagnostics in Reading the Data.

2. Setup

  • Dataset: the Kaggle Titanic training set, train.csv (891 passengers). Locally it lives at course_materials/Data/Titanic data/train.csv (note the space in the folder name), so a loader call is pd.read_csv("course_materials/Data/Titanic data/train.csv"). The stub itself names only the bare filename train.csv and gives no loader.
  • Columns referenced by the prompt: Fare, Sex, Embarked, Survived (the 0/1 target), and passenger class. The actual CSV header is PassengerId, Survived, Pclass, Name, Sex, Age, SibSp, Parch, Ticket, Fare, Cabin, Embarked (verified against the local file).
  • Target (implied): Survived (0/1) — the survival-probability questions are means of this column.
  • Task: preprocessing + descriptive analysis. No train/test split, no estimator, no fit.
  • Expected output: a cleaned DataFrame, a stated imputation/outlier strategy, encoded categoricals, and computed survival probabilities by class and by sex, plus a short written discussion of what passenger class represents and why preprocessing matters.

The stub writes pclass (lowercase); the real column is Pclass. The Kaggle CSV capitalizes Pclass, Survived, Sex, Embarked, and Fare. Code that hard-codes the lowercase pclass from the prompt raises KeyError. Always check the header with df.columns before indexing. (Verified against the local train.csv header.)

3. Walkthrough

There is no _VHL solution notebook for this topic — the stub is a single markdown cell with a seven-part prompt and no code. The walkthrough below is therefore a sketch of the standard decisions the prompt asks for, anchored to the Reading the Data diagnostics rather than to stored solution cells. No numbers or outputs are reproduced because none exist to cite.

Decision 1 — load and inspect (prompt part 1). df.head(), df.info(), df.describe(). The point of info() here is to surface the missingness: it prints non-null counts per column, which is how you discover that Age, Cabin, and Embarked are incomplete — see Diagnostic 2 — Missingness.

Decision 2 — impute missing values (prompt part 2). Three columns, three different calls:

  • Age (~177 missing) — fill with the median, not the mean, because the age distribution is right-skewed and the median is robust to that skew: df["Age"].fillna(df["Age"].median()).
  • Embarked (a couple missing) — fill with the mode (most common port): df["Embarked"].fillna(df["Embarked"].mode()[0]).
  • Cabin (~77% missing) — too sparse to impute; the usual move is to drop the column rather than invent values.

The key idiom is choosing the statistic to match the column’s type and shape (median for skewed numeric, mode for categorical) — not a single blanket rule.

Decision 3 — outliers on Fare (prompt part 3). Fare is heavily right-skewed (a handful of very high first-class fares). Inspect with df["Fare"].describe() or a boxplot, then decide: cap at a high quantile (winsorize) or leave them, but do not blindly delete — the extreme fares are legitimate first-class tickets that carry real survival signal. The decision and its justification are what the prompt grades.

Decision 4 — encode categoricals (prompt part 4). Sex is binary → label-encode to 0/1 (df["Sex"].map({"male": 0, "female": 1})). Embarked is nominal with three levels → one-hot encode (pd.get_dummies(df, columns=["Embarked"])), which produces the C/Q/S indicator columns. The idiom worth internalizing: binary → label, multi-level nominal → one-hot.

Decision 5 — survival probabilities (prompt parts 5–6). Because Survived is 0/1, a survival probability is just its group mean:

df.groupby("Pclass")["Survived"].mean()    # survival rate by class
df.groupby("Sex")["Survived"].mean()        # survival rate male vs female

This is the conceptual heart of the exercise: a conditional probability P(Survived | group) read directly off the data as a groupby(...).mean(). The written parts (6–7) then ask what Pclass represents (a socio-economic proxy: 1st/2nd/3rd) and why this cleaning matters before any model.

4. Common pitfalls

  • Mean vs median for Age. Using the mean on a skewed column pulls imputed values toward the long tail; median is the safer default. (See Diagnostic 2.)
  • Deleting Fare outliers wholesale discards real first-class passengers and biases survival rates downward for that group.
  • Lowercase pclass — the prompt’s spelling does not match the CSV’s Pclass; will KeyError.
  • Forgetting that Survived is binary — “survival probability” is mean() of the 0/1 column; computing a count or a sum instead answers a different question.
  • One-hot vs label for Embarked — label-encoding a 3-level nominal (S=0, C=1, Q=2) invents a false ordering; use one-hot. Sex is the exception because it is binary.
  • Imputing Cabin — at ~77% missing there is nothing reliable to impute; drop it.

5. What this exercise teaches that the others don’t

It is the only drill focused purely on raw-data preprocessing and EDA — missing-value imputation, outlier treatment, and categorical encoding — with no estimator at all. It is also the only one that asks you to compute conditional probabilities directly from a real tabular dataset as descriptive statistics (groupby().mean() on a 0/1 target), rather than getting them out of a fitted classifier. Every modelling drill silently assumes these steps are already done; this one makes them the whole job.
